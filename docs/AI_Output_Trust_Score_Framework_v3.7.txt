1. Executive Summary 
The AI Output Trust Score is a single, defensible number — out of 100 — that tells a reviewer whether an 
AI-generated analytical output can be trusted, used, escalated, or rejected. It exists because the difference 
between a usable AI deliverable and an unusable one is no longer about fluency; it is about whether the 
output is grounded in evidence, follows the agreed methodology, conforms to the agreed structure, and 
exposes its own assumptions and uncertainties. 
This framework is purpose-built to evaluate outputs produced through the modular prompting 
architecture described in the supplied source PDF. That architecture separates what changes (Variables) 
from what is fixed (Module 1 Research Standard, Module 2 Methodology, Module 3 Output Structure). 
Because the prompt itself enforces a quality bar, a methodology, and a deliverable format, evaluators can 
— and must — score the resulting output against those exact same anchors. 
The framework converts the four failures that the PDF identifies in single-shot prompting (no quality 
standard, no methodology, no output structure, no data anchoring) into five weighted scoring categories 
that together total 100 points. Reviewers apply a 0–5 rubric within each category, multiply by the category 
weight, and sum to a Trust Score. The Trust Score then maps to one of five interpretation bands ranging 
from Very High Trust (90–100) to Very Low Trust (0–39), each with an associated usage decision and 
remediation pathway. 
Why this framework exists 
Single-shot prompts produce outputs that look polished but cannot be trusted, compared, or 
repeated. The modular prompting framework fixes the prompt side. This Trust Score framework 
fixes the evaluation side — so that the same output, reviewed by two different people, lands at 
substantially the same score. 
2. Purpose and Scope 
This framework applies to AI-generated analytical outputs that were produced using a modular prompt 
of the form described in the source PDF — that is, a prompt composed of an editable Variables block plus 
three fixed modules (Research Standard, Methodology, Output Structure). 
In scope 
• Financial-analysis outputs of the kinds illustrated in the PDF: Market Snapshot, Commodity 
Review, Financial Health, Portfolio Checkup, and Macro Backdrop. 
• Any other analytical deliverable generated through the same modular prompt template, where 
Variables, Research Standard, Methodology, and Output Structure can be identified. 
• Outputs intended for downstream business use — investment memos, internal review packs, 
client-facing summaries, or analyst handoffs.

Out of scope (and why)

• Code generation, translation, and other non-analytical tasks. Reviewers should use a task-

• Quality assurance reviewers and AI governance teams, as the primary scorers. 
• Prompt engineers, as a feedback loop into module design. 
• Compliance reviewers, who rely on the score band to decide whether a human sign-off is 
required. 
• Business stakeholders and analysts consuming the output, as a single, transparent quality signal. 
3. Evaluation Principles 
Every score awarded under this framework should follow the seven principles below. They exist to make 
scores defensible, comparable, and resistant to reviewer drift. 
Principle 
What it means in practice 
Objectivity 
Score the output on the page, not the reputation of the model, the 
analyst, or the company under analysis. A confident tone is not 
evidence; a citation is. 
Traceability 
Every point awarded or withheld must be tied to a specific observation 
in the output (a sentence, a table cell, a missing section). Reviewer 
notes must record where the evidence was — or was not — found. 
Prompt alignment 
The prompt is the contract. Score adherence to the Variables, the 
Research Standard, the Methodology, and the Output Structure as 
supplied — not to an idealised prompt the reviewer wishes had been 
used. 
Evidence-based scoring 
Claims without sourced evidence are penalised in Category 3. Claims 
with sourced evidence that does not actually support them are 
penalised more heavily — fabricated sourcing is worse than no sourcing.

5. Detailed Scoring Framework 
Each of the five subsections below specifies, for one category: the score weighting, the criteria, the 0–5 
rubric, three illustrative examples, the reviewer checklist, common failure modes, red flags, and 
consistency guidance. Reviewers should work through them in order; Section 7 provides a single 
consolidated checklist for use during review. 
5.1 Category 1: Prompt Adherence and Instruction Compliance 
Field 
Detail 
Maximum score 
20 points 
Purpose 
Measures the degree to which the AI output respects the seven editable 
Variables and the explicit instructions supplied in the prompt (Company, 
Identifier, Coverage Period, Peer Set, Purpose & Audience, Data 
Sources, Accounting/Currency Notes). 
Why it matters for trust 
The Variables block is the only part of the prompt that defines what the 
user actually asked for. If the output drifts from the Variables — wrong 
company, wrong period, wrong peer set, wrong audience register — 
every subsequent strength is wasted. Adherence is the foundation of 
every other score. 
 
Detailed scoring criteria 
• The output addresses the exact Company and Identifier supplied; no substitution, no implicit 
broadening. 
• The Coverage Period is respected — no smuggling-in of out-of-scope quarters or years. 
• The Peer Set used in comparisons matches the supplied list; no silent additions or omissions. 
• The Purpose & Audience drives tone, depth, and emphasis (e.g., a CFO audience is treated 
differently from a retail-investor audience). 
• Only the supplied Data Sources are used; if the output draws on others, it must say so explicitly. 
• Accounting/Currency Notes (e.g., USD vs. local currency, IFRS vs. US GAAP) are observed and 
applied throughout.

Reviewer checklist questions 
• Are all seven Variables explicitly observable in the output? 
• Is the Coverage Period visibly enforced section-by-section? 
• Does the Peer Set in the Peer Context section match the supplied list exactly? 
• Does the tone and depth match the Purpose & Audience field? 
• Is the currency and accounting basis consistent throughout? 
• Are any prompt-level restrictions (exclusions, scope limits) honoured? 
• Where the output departs from the prompt, is the departure flagged and justified? 
Common failure modes 
• Silent substitution of an entity (e.g., parent for subsidiary) without disclosure. 
• Period drift — switching from the stated Coverage Period to a more convenient one inside the 
analysis. 
• Peer-set inflation, where extra comparable companies are added 'for completeness'. 
• Audience mismatch, producing executive-summary tone for a technical credit-committee request, 
or vice versa. 
Red flags that should reduce the score 
• Output ignores a stated restriction (e.g., 'do not include forecasts') and includes the restricted 
content anyway. 
• Currency or unit changes mid-document without an explicit note. 
• Generic boilerplate that does not reference the supplied Variables at all. 
How to apply this score consistently 
Score Category 1 first, before reading the body of the output for analysis quality. Cross-check each 
Variable against the relevant section of the output and tick it off explicitly in your reviewer notes. If 
three or more Variables are not visibly respected, the category cannot score above 3 regardless of the 
analytical quality elsewhere.

5.5 Category 5: Currency, Risk Awareness, Restrictions, and Transparency 
Field 
Detail 
Maximum score 
15 points 
Purpose 
Measures whether the output handles time, uncertainty, and prompt-
level restrictions transparently. Anchored to Module 1 — Research 
Standard: data recency checks, explicit assumptions, calibrated 
language, and red-flag surfacing. 
Why it matters for trust 
Even a fully accurate output becomes untrustworthy if it presents stale 
data as current, hides its assumptions, ignores stated restrictions, or 
fails to surface red flags. This category captures the 'meta-quality' of the 
output — its honesty about its own limitations. 
 
Detailed scoring criteria 
• Data recency is explicit — every key data point is dated. 
• Where data is older than the Coverage Period would imply, the staleness is flagged. 
• All material assumptions are stated up front, not buried. 
• Red flags identified during analysis are surfaced in the Risks & Red Flags section, not soft-pedalled 
in passing. 
• Restrictions stated in the prompt (e.g., 'do not provide investment advice') are honoured visibly. 
• Limitations of the analysis (data gaps, peer comparability issues, accounting differences) are 
disclosed. 
• Forward-looking statements use calibrated, conditional language rather than asserting the future 
as fact.

Reviewer checklist questions 
• Is every key data point dated? 
• Where data is older than the Coverage Period, is the staleness flagged? 
• Are material assumptions stated up front? 
• Are red flags surfaced in the Risks section, not buried in pillars? 
• Are all stated prompt restrictions visibly honoured? 
• Are analytical limitations (data gaps, peer comparability) disclosed? 
• Is forward-looking language calibrated and conditional? 
Common failure modes 
• Date-free framing — 'currently' or 'as of today' without an actual date. 
• Hidden assumptions — key inputs (e.g., FX rate, growth assumption) not stated. 
• Red-flag suppression — issues acknowledged inside the analysis but not elevated to Risks. 
• Restriction creep — output drifts past stated restrictions section by section. 
Red flags that should reduce the score 
• Output presents data older than the Coverage Period as if it were current, with no flag. 
• An explicit prompt restriction is breached. 
• Forward-looking statements asserted as fact ('will', 'definitely') without supporting evidence. 
How to apply this score consistently 
Score this category last, after the analytical categories. Many of its failures are visible only once the 
reviewer knows what the analysis actually concluded, for example, a missing red flag is recognisable only 
when the underlying data has been checked. Use the Trust Score's overall direction as a sanity check: a 
strong analytical output with weak transparency often deserves a band downgrade rather than a small 
deduction here.

If formatting issues are cosmetic (font, spacing), do not deduct. If they

strong facts 
Deduct in Category 4. A factually correct but analytically shallow 
output is a Category 4 problem, not a Category 3 problem. 
Overconfident conclusions 
Deduct in Category 4 (calibrated-language criterion). Use Category 5 
only if overconfidence is paired with hidden assumptions. 
Reviewer disagreement 
Escalate to a second reviewer rather than averaging. If the second 
reviewer's score is in a different band, the lower band applies and the 
case is logged for the next calibration cycle.

Note: the cadences and mechanisms in this section are professional assumptions, not derived from the 
source PDF. The PDF describes the modular prompting framework but does not prescribe its own 
governance. 
Ownership 
• A single named owner — typically AI Governance or QA leadership — owns the framework's 
content, version history, and calibration log. 
• Prompt engineering owns the modular prompt template (Variables, Module 1, Module 2, Module 
3); coordination between the two owners is required whenever either changes. 
Calibration cadence 
• Quarterly: a calibration session in which two reviewers independently score the same five recent 
outputs. Score gaps greater than one band trigger a guidance update. 
• Ad-hoc: any time the modular prompt template adds, removes, or renames a Variable or a 
Module section, the framework is reviewed to confirm category criteria still align. 
Versioning 
• The framework is versioned (v1.0, v1.1, v2.0). Major versions reflect category changes; minor 
versions reflect rubric or guidance refinement. 
• Each output's Trust Score should record the framework version under which it was scored, so 
historical comparisons remain meaningful. 
Continuous improvement signals 
• Recurring failure patterns observed in reviewer notes feed back into the prompt template — 
typically into Module 1 (Research Standard) or Module 3 (Output Structure). 
• Rubric levels that produce frequent reviewer disagreement are candidates for sharper criteria or 
worked examples. 
• Override rules are reviewed annually; new override candidates require evidence of repeated, 
high-consequence failure. 
11. Final Summary 
The AI Output Trust Score Evaluation Framework converts the modular prompting architecture in the 
source PDF into an evaluation contract. Where the prompt template enforces a quality bar, a 
methodology, and a deliverable format on the input side, this framework enforces the matching standards 
on the output side, using five weighted categories that together total 100 points. 
By scoring Prompt Adherence, Structural Alignment, Accuracy and Evidence, Reasoning Quality, and 
Currency and Transparency separately and explicitly, the framework gives reviewers a defensible audit 
trail, gives prompt engineers a feedback loop, and gives business stakeholders a single trust signal that

maps directly to a usage decision. The interpretation bands and override rules ensure that the most 
consequential failures, fabricated sources, restriction breaches, cannot be averaged away by surface 
polish elsewhere. 
Used consistently, the framework improves three things at once: confidence in individual outputs, 
accountability across the AI delivery chain, and consistency between reviewers, periods, and use cases. It 
is intended to evolve alongside the modular prompting template itself, with calibration and versioning as 
standing practices rather than one-off projects. 
Bottom line 
Modular prompting fixes how AI outputs are produced. The Trust Score framework fixes how they 
are evaluated. Together, they make analytical AI deliverables comparable, defensible, and fit for 
downstream business use.
