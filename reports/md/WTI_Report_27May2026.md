Structured fundamentals assessment. Each factor carries a direction label and a structural / cyclical tag.
**Supply — price-supportive (cyclical, with structural overlay).**
The dominant supply story remains the Strait of Hormuz disruption and the associated record drawdown in global crude inventories. Industry reporting through May described the inventory draw as the worst supply disruption on record, with the IEA having dialled back its earlier oversupply warning. US crude stocks fell by 7.9 million barrels in the week ending 15 May per published API/EIA-cycle reporting, while US exports ran at record highs. OPEC+ supply management is in flux: the UAE's exit from OPEC and OPEC+ took effect on 1 May, adding uncertainty to the cartel's supply discipline. This factor is price-supportive and is the principal reason the bearish case is not stronger. It is partly cyclical (the Hormuz bottleneck reverses if the strait reopens) and partly structural (record-low inventories take months to rebuild).
**Demand — neutral to mildly price-negative (cyclical).**
Demand has been eroded by the higher price environment of March–April and by broader economic headwinds. The same elevated prices that reflect the supply shock are themselves rationing consumption. Memorial Day US gasoline prices were the highest in four years, a demand-dampening signal at the margin. On balance demand is a mild headwind, not a driver — cyclical and reversible within the scenario horizon.
**Substitution and spreads — neutral (cyclical).**
Against the comparison set — Brent crude, heating oil, natural gas and RBOB gasoline — WTI is trading at a wide discount to Brent (Brent above USD 100 while WTI sits in the low-to-mid 90s), reflecting the global-versus-US dynamic: the Hormuz disruption is felt more acutely in the international Brent basket. Refined-product cracks (gasoline, heating oil) moved with crude during the week. The wide Brent–WTI spread signals relative US adequacy and is a mild structural cap on WTI's upside relative to Brent.
**Policy and regulation — price-supportive but two-directional (cyclical).**
US–Iran ceasefire diplomacy is the swing policy variable. A framework to extend the ceasefire by roughly two months — under which Washington would ease its blockade and Tehran would reopen Hormuz — is under negotiation. Progress is price-negative for crude (it unwinds the war premium); a breakdown is sharply price-positive. Sanctions policy on Russian seaborne oil has also been in motion, with a waiver allowed to expire. This factor is the single largest source of two-way risk in the forecast.
**Macro, logistics and FX — mildly price-supportive (cyclical).**
The US dollar is soft (Dollar Index around 98.2), which is a mild tailwind for dollar-priced crude. Front-month curve structure reflects steep backwardation — the forward strip falls month-on-month from the low-90s front-month to the high-70s by year-end — confirming that the market prices the current tightness as temporary. Backwardation is a positioning headwind for passive long exposure (negative roll yield) even as it confirms physical tightness.
**Near-term catalysts — see §13d.**
The EIA Weekly Petroleum Status Report on Wednesday 27 May is the immediate catalyst and coincides with this report's date. US–Iran negotiation headlines are a continuous catalyst with no fixed schedule. Both are flagged in the upcoming-period calendar (§13d) and weighted against the transitional regime: in a transitional regime, a catalyst surprise is more likely to resolve the direction of the next break than to be absorbed.
